The historical difficulty of establishing permanent human settlements in the Tundra region stems from several severe environmental challenges unique to the polar climate:

1.  **Extreme and Prolonged Cold:** The Tundra region is characterized by being "extremely cold." During the winter months, temperatures are "bitterly cold," making continuous survival difficult and requiring specialized methods for heat and protection.
2.  **Extended Darkness and Icy Conditions:** During the winter (specifically cited as November, December, and January), the sun does not rise at all, meaning the region is "almost dark throughout" these months. This lack of light, combined with the extreme cold, creates "dark, deserted and desolate" conditions.
3.  **Permafrost and Lack of Deep Soil:** The underlying ground is permanently frozen. The "upper soil surface of the region is frozen into a rock-like state all through the year," which is called **permafrost**. This hard, frozen underground soil makes deep root growth difficult and limits farming or permanent construction methods.
4.  **Treeless Landscape and Strong Winds:** Due to the combination of little soil, permafrost, and severe weather, the Tundra region is mostly treeless. Strong cold winds and storms damage and uproot any trees that might manage to grow, eliminating wood as a source for stable building materials and fuel.
5.  **Seasonal Instability of Food Resources:** Due to the severe winter conditions, plants die off. Furthermore, many birds and animals "leave this region and migrate elsewhere" during the cold months, leading to a highly unstable and fluctuating food supply that historically necessitated a nomadic lifestyle (hunting and fishing) rather than permanent settlement.
